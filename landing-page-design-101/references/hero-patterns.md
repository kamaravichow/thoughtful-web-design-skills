# Hero patterns — headlines, subcopy, and the first screen

Read this when writing or rewriting a hero section, or when a page fails the five-second test.

## What a hero has to do

Four jobs, in order:

1. **Name the outcome or the problem.** Headline.
2. **Say who it's for and how it works.** Subcopy, one or two lines.
3. **Give one obvious next action.** Primary CTA.
4. **Reduce risk.** Proof — a named customer, a number, a recognisable logo, a photo of the actual work.

If any of the four is missing, the hero is doing less than its job. If there are more than four, it's doing too much.

## Headline formulas that hold up

These aren't templates to paste — they're shapes that force specificity.

- **[Outcome] for [specific audience].**
  → "Payroll for restaurants with under 50 staff."
- **[Do the hard thing] without [the usual cost].**
  → "Ship your brand site without hiring an agency."
- **The [category] that [differentiator].**
  → "The CRM that fills itself in from your inbox."
- **[Verb] [the thing] in [timeframe].**
  → "Get your accounts filed in ten days."
- **Direct claim + proof in the subcopy.**
  → "We build sites that pay for themselves." / "Average client sees a 40% lift in enquiries within 90 days."

## Before / after

**Before:** "Crafting immersive digital experiences."
**After:** "Websites for law firms that turn traffic into consultations."
*Why:* the first describes the vendor's self-image. The second describes the buyer's outcome.

**Before:** "Reimagining the future of work."
**After:** "Shift scheduling for teams that hate spreadsheets."
*Why:* the abstraction was doing zero identification work. The rewrite tells the wrong visitor to leave, which is a feature.

**Before:** "Innovative solutions, tailored to you."
**After:** "Bookkeeping for UK construction firms. Reconciled every Friday."
*Why:* "tailored" is unfalsifiable. A day of the week is a promise.

**Before:** "Design that moves."
**After:** "Product launch pages, built in two weeks."
*Why:* the pun was the point of the original. Puns are for taglines, not headlines — and a tagline is optional.

## Subcopy rules

- One or two lines. If it needs three, the headline is underperforming.
- Answer the objection the headline raises. Headline promises speed → subcopy explains how it's possible.
- Concrete nouns and numbers. Vague intensifiers ("seamlessly," "effortlessly," "truly") can be deleted with no loss of meaning, which is the test.

## CTA rules

- One primary action, visually dominant. A secondary action is fine as a text link, not a second button of equal weight.
- Label the outcome, not the mechanism: "Get a quote" beats "Submit." "See pricing" beats "Learn more."
- Repeat the same CTA down the page rather than introducing new ones. Consistency reduces decision cost.
- Put the risk-reducer next to the button: "No card required," "Reply within one working day."

## Proof that works

Ranked roughly by strength:

1. A named customer with a photograph and a specific result.
2. A concrete number tied to a claim.
3. A short quote with full name, role, company.
4. Recognisable client logos.
5. Star ratings and review counts.
6. Anonymous testimonials — barely counts.

The Highrise result is the canonical illustration: a large photo of a real customer with a direct quote and a clear headline roughly doubled sign-ups against an elaborate, screenshot-heavy alternative. Human faces and specific claims outperform feature grids.

## Anti-patterns

- **The mystery hero.** Full-bleed abstract visual, three-word tagline, no indication of the industry. Beautiful, useless.
- **Feature soup.** Six benefits at equal weight above the fold. Equal emphasis is no emphasis.
- **The gated intro.** Loader or animation that must complete before the headline is readable. This is a paywall made of vanity.
- **Copy over motion.** Text animating over moving video is unreadable in exactly the moment it matters most.
- **Nav overload.** Nine top-level links competing with the CTA.
